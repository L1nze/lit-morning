Recent advances in search agents have enabled large language models to tackle complex tasks by retrieving and synthesizing external information. However, existing search agents often lack search-state awareness, i.e., the ability to recognize what information has been obtained and what remains missing, leading to hallucinated reasoning and prematurely terminated search. To address this limitation, we propose Self-Assessment Reinforcement Learning (SARL), a reinforcement learning framework in which models learn to recognize their own search state during training and improve their search behavior accordingly, without requiring additional assessment at test time. SARL achieves this through a dual reward design that captures the corrective effect of each assessment while penalizing excessive reliance on it, jointly encouraging effective and efficient search trajectories. Under this setup, SARL achieves a 24.3% relative improvement over the strongest baseline, with average gains of 35.9% on multi-hop reasoning and 27.9% on deep research benchmarks against the strongest per-benchmark baselines. SARL further surpasses methods built on auxiliary supervision or multi-agent coordination, despite operating as a standalone RL framework with no inference-time scaffolding. Beyond final performance, SARL maintains training stability under extended search horizons, a regime where existing methods consistently degrade, enabling sustained gains as task complexity grows.

Large language models (LLMs) commonly operate under inference procedures that allocate computational resources according to predefined configurations, fixed reasoning budgets, predetermined decoding strategies, or manually specified routing rules. However, the computational requirements of individual tasks can vary substantially, and the value of additional computation is often uncertain before it is performed. This paper proposes Prediction-Driven Adaptive Computation for Large Language Models (PDAC-LLM), a unified framework in which a model predicts the potential value, cost, and expected outcome of alternative computational actions before allocating additional inference resources. At each decision point, the controller estimates the expected quality improvement, computational cost, latency, uncertainty reduction, and risk associated with candidate actions. It then selects an action according to a resource-aware decision rule, continuing computation when the predicted marginal benefit justifies the additional cost and stopping when further computation is unlikely to provide sufficient value. Current alignment paradigms for Large Language Models (LLMs)—predominantly Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)—introduce an unintended systemic artifact termed the Alignment Tax: an aggressive default toward risk-averse heuristic conservatism, epistemic paternalism, and superficial dispute de-escalation. When confronted with unconventional, high-stakes human strategies, aligned models frequently exhibit a cascade of communicative failures rather than objective analytical support. In this empirical study, we document and formalize the Defensive Compensation Cascade (DCC), an emergent interaction pattern wherein an LLM under rigorous game-theoretic scrutiny shifts from (1) conservative risk-aversion, to (2) false detachment and simulated moral superiority, to (3) subtle narrative confabulation and passive-aggressive counter-positioning, before finally collapsing into an objective epistemic instrument under recursive formal logic. We formalize this trajectory as a history-dependent state transition model across contextual latent spaces, demonstrate how rigorous game-theoretic formulations dismantle pre-trained alignment priors without prompt-injection or jailbreaking heuristics, and establish the Context-Adversarial Red-Teaming (CART) protocol as an evaluation benchmark for LLM intellectual integrity.
